## 1. Rice Staples

### Braised Pork Rice (Lu Rou Fan)

**What it is:** Minced or small-cut pork belly braised in soy, sugar and spices, served over steamed rice. Styles vary: some are glossy and gelatinous, others lighter and more savory.

**What to order:** A small bowl of braised pork rice, plus a side of boiled greens, braised tofu or a soup if you are hungry.

**Taste and texture:** Savory, slightly sweet, fatty but usually balanced with rice. The pork should be tender, not dry.

### Chicken Rice

**What it is:** Poached or steamed chicken served over rice, often with a light soy sauce or ginger-scallion oil. The dish looks simple, but quality depends on the chicken and rice.

**What to order:** Chicken rice with a small soup or vegetable side. Ask whether the sauce is light soy or ginger-scallion oil if you prefer one style.

**Taste and texture:** Clean chicken flavor, tender meat, fragrant rice; less heavy than braised pork rice.

**Where to look:** Night markets, local diners and chicken-rice stalls often serve it by portion or half/quarter chicken. Check the map for a shop near your itinerary rather than choosing a tourist-only spot.

### Tainan-Style Rice Bowl and Southern Taiwanese Rice Dishes

**What it is:** Southern Taiwan, especially Tainan, has a strong rice-bowl tradition with savory-sweet braised toppings, shrimp, pork or fish.

**What to know:** Tainan flavors tend to be sweeter and more old-school than Taipei-style food. If you visit southern Taiwan, compare a local rice bowl with Taipei braised pork rice rather than assuming they taste the same.

## 2. Noodle Soups and Dumplings

### Beef Noodle Soup (Niu Rou Mian)

**What it is:** Stewed beef served with noodles in broth. Broth styles range from light and herbal to rich, soy-spiced and spicy. Noodles may be thick, thin or hand-cut.

**What to order:** Start with a classic beef noodle soup. Choose non-spicy if you do not eat chili. Ask for more greens or an extra noodle portion if available.

**Taste and texture:** Rich broth, tender beef, chewy noodles. Some versions include radish or pickled vegetables.

### Vermicelli Soup (Mian Xian)

**What it is:** Thin rice vermicelli in broth, commonly paired with pork intestine, clams, oysters or shredded pork.

**What to order:** Choose a basic intestine vermicelli or oyster vermicelli. If you avoid shellfish, ask for a pork or intestine version instead.

**Taste and texture:** Soft, absorbent noodles; broth often savory with garlic or five-spice notes.

**Where to look:** Old markets, night markets and traditional snack shops. In Day 1’s Taipei itinerary, Minle Street and Raohe Market are examples of areas where vermicelli appears on local menus.

### Dumplings and Pan-Fried Buns (Shui Jiao / Sheng Jian Bao)

**What it is:** Dumplings may be boiled, pan-fried or steamed. Common fillings are pork, cabbage, chive or seafood. Pan-fried buns often have a crispy bottom and soft dough top.

**What to order:** A plate of boiled or pan-fried dumplings, plus a soup if you want a fuller meal.

**Taste and texture:** Juicy filling, chewy wrapper; dipping sauce usually combines soy, vinegar, garlic or chili.

## 3. Night-Market and Street Snacks

### Fried Chicken Cutlet (Ji Pai)

**What it is:** A large flattened chicken cutlet coated in seasoned batter and deep-fried. It is one of the most recognizable Taiwanese night-market snacks.

**What to order:** One cutlet to share. Ask for less pepper or no chili if you are sensitive to spice.

**Taste and texture:** Crispy outside, juicy inside, seasoned with pepper, salt, garlic or basil.

## 4. Breakfast and Small Plates

### Egg Pancake (Dan Bing)

**What it is:** A Taiwanese breakfast egg wrap made from a soft flatbread and egg. It may be plain or filled with ham, cheese, vegetables or basil.

**What to order:** Plain egg pancake, egg pancake with cheese, or basil egg pancake. Pair it with hot or cold soy milk.

**Taste and texture:** Soft, slightly chewy, mild egg flavor; not the same as a Western omelet.

### Soy Milk (Dou Jiang)

**What it is:** Soy milk served hot or cold, sweet or unsweetened. It is a standard Taiwanese breakfast drink.

**What to order:** Ask for unsweetened if you do not like sweet drinks. Sweetened soy milk is common at traditional breakfast shops.

### Sesame Bread and Fried Dough Stick (Shao Bing / You Tiao)

**What it is:** Shao Bing is a baked sesame bread; youtiao is a fried dough stick. They are often eaten together, torn into soy milk, or served as a sandwich.

**What to order:** Sesame bread with youtiao, or sesame bread with egg, plus soy milk.

## 5. Drinks and Desserts

### Bubble Tea / Pearl Milk Tea (Zhen Nai Nai Cha)

**What it is:** Taiwan’s famous milk tea with chewy tapioca pearls. Tea drinks usually allow choices for sweetness and ice level.

**What to order:** Start with half sugar or less sugar, normal or less ice if unsure. Classic options include black sugar milk tea, pearl milk tea and fresh milk versions.

**Taste and texture:** Sweet tea and milk with chewy pearls; sweetness varies strongly by shop.

**What to know:** Pearls are chewy, not hard candy. Some shops use fresh milk, others creamer; ask if you have dietary preferences.

### Tofu Pudding (Dou Hua)

**What it is:** Soft tofu pudding served with sweet syrup, soy milk or toppings such as taro balls, red beans, mung beans, peanuts or grass jelly.

**What to order:** Plain tofu pudding with one or two toppings. Ask whether the base is syrup or soy milk.

**Taste and texture:** Silky and soft, lightly sweet; toppings add texture and flavor.

### Mango Shaved Ice and Snowflake Ice

**What it is:** Shaved or snowflake ice topped with fresh mango, condensed milk, ice cream or pudding. It is a signature Taiwanese summer dessert.

**What to order:** Mango ice in mango season; if mango is out of season, choose another fruit, bean or tea-flavored ice.

## How to Order Comfortably

1. **Start small.** Choose one staple, one side dish and one drink so you can try more places during the trip.
2. **Ask about spice and allergens.** Common allergens include peanut, sesame, seafood, egg, wheat and chili.
3. **State dietary restrictions early.** If you do not eat beef, pork, seafood or dairy, mention it before ordering rather than after the food arrives.
4. **Carry cash.** Traditional markets, snack stalls and many night-market vendors prefer cash; card acceptance varies.
5. **Watch local customers.** Follow the queue order, observe where people sit, and copy how regulars order if the menu is unclear.
6. **Adjust sweetness and ice.** Tea drinks and desserts can often be adjusted; half sugar is a safe first choice if you do not like very sweet food.

## Food Restrictions

### Vegetarian

Look for vegetable buns, tofu dishes, mushroom noodles, egg dishes and convenience-store vegetarian options. Buddhist vegetarian restaurants may avoid garlic and onion; ask if that matters to you.

### No Beef

Beef noodle soup, beef soup and some broths may contain beef stock. Ask clearly whether the soup base contains beef before ordering.

### Seafood Allergies

Oyster omelet, vermicelli with clams, sashimi and some sauces may contain shellfish or fish. Confirm ingredients directly with the stall.

### Peanut Sensitivity

Pork blood cake, some fried snacks, desserts and sauces may contain peanut powder or peanut oil. Ask before choosing peanut-topped dishes.

## Practical Food Notes

1. **Portions can be shared.** Many fried snacks and desserts are easier to try when split between two people.
2. **Breakfast culture starts early.** Traditional soy-milk shops often open in the morning and sell out popular items before afternoon.
3. **Night markets are dinner plus snacking.** Do not arrive too full; order two or three small dishes rather than one large meal.
4. **Southern and northern flavors differ.** Tainan and other southern cities often favor sweeter, old-style savory dishes; Taipei has a wider mix of regional and modern food.
